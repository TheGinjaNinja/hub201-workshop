# Customer Interview Methodology

Adapted from Jamie Reynolds' customer discovery guide, which credits David J. Bland and Alexander Osterwalder's Testing Business Ideas as a primary source. Also draws on Teresa Torres' Continuous Discovery Habits.

## Preparation

**Narrow your target customer.** Do not interview everyone. Pick one specific segment at a time: a job title, a company stage, a vertical, a geography. Broad targeting produces diffuse insights that are hard to act on. Sharp targeting produces patterns you can build from.

**10-20 candidates minimum per segment.** Fewer than 10 and you cannot distinguish individual quirks from real patterns. More than 20 and you are usually getting diminishing returns before you synthesise. Run 5 interviews, do a quick synthesis, then decide whether to continue or pivot the segment.

**Timeframe: 2 weeks to 2 months.** Under 2 weeks and you are rushing the recruiting and the synthesis. Over 2 months and the market is moving while you interview. Most discovery sprints should complete a round of 10-15 interviews within 3 to 4 weeks.

**In-person vs remote.** Remote is faster to schedule, recordable without friction, and cheaper. It is the right default. In-person lets you observe body language and workspace context, which is worth prioritising for complex B2B decisions or when the physical environment is part of the problem. Do not treat in-person as inherently more rigorous; a well-run remote interview produces equally strong data.

## Mindset

**Stay objective.** The strongest pull in discovery is toward confirmation. You have a hypothesis; you want it to be right; you unconsciously steer the interview toward answers that support it. Every leading question is a symptom of this. The discipline is to push for the why behind every answer, even when the first answer supports your hypothesis.

**Do not pitch.** The founder's instinct, especially when the conversation is going well, is to start describing the solution. Resist it. The moment you pitch, you have converted a discovery interview into a sales call. The respondent switches mode too: instead of reporting their reality, they start evaluating your idea. The data degrades instantly.

**The failure rate context.** 99% of startups fail because they are solutions looking for a problem. The product was built before the problem was understood clearly enough. Discovery is the mechanism that prevents that. Every hour spent in a well-run interview before building is worth more than a week of iteration after.

## Script skeleton

Structure every discovery script in five sections. The order matters: start wide and contextual, narrow toward specific pain and behaviour, end with the open invitation.

**Intro (3-4 questions):** Establish who the person is and give them a chance to talk about themselves. Job title, main responsibilities, tenure in the role, how they ended up there. This is rapport-building, but it is also real data: tenure and responsibility scope affect how much authority they have and how much of the problem they own.

**Industry (2-3 questions):** Zoom out to the sector. Biggest challenge for the industry this year. Biggest opportunity. Trends they are watching. This surfaces the macro context the person is operating inside and often reveals problem areas you had not considered.

**Business (4-6 questions):** Narrow to their organisation. Biggest business challenge, pain points by phase of the business journey, procurement process for new tools, most recent software purchase. The procurement and software purchase questions are particularly load-bearing: they tell you how decisions are made, who is involved, and whether there is precedent for buying products like yours.

**Personal (4-6 questions):** Narrow further to their individual experience. Most common tasks, self-rated ability on those tasks, personal pain points, data sources they rely on, trends affecting their specific role. This is where the most actionable product insight lives: the person in front of you, doing their actual job, hitting the actual friction.

**Outro:** The magic wand question. "If you had a magic wand and could fix one problem, what would it be?" This is the most unguarded moment of the interview. The person has been talking about their world for 30-45 minutes; their defences are down; they are not trying to be polite or manage impressions. The answer is often the most honest thing they say.

## The Value Proposition Canvas lens

Every question in the script should probe one of three things: jobs (what the person is trying to get done), pains (what makes those jobs harder or more frustrating), or gains (what would make the job better or easier). This is the Value Proposition Canvas framework.

Before finalising a script, review each question and label it jobs, pains, or gains. A script that is all pains and no gains will miss the aspirational dimension. A script that is all jobs but no pains will miss the problem. Check the balance.

## The Persona Canvas lens

Use this lens for questions about who the person is and how they buy. The Persona Canvas covers six dimensions: profile (demographics, role), personality (how they think and decide), trends (forces affecting them), channels (how they receive information), products and services (what they already use), and communications (how they prefer to be reached).

Discovery interviews do not need to map all six dimensions explicitly, but the lens is useful when reviewing the script to check you have not ignored the buyer profile. Knowing their personality type and preferred channels matters when you later design distribution and sales.

## Recording and consent

Always ask permission before recording. Treat the contents of every interview as confidential. Breaking trust with an interviewee, even informally or "just with the team", will cost you future access and damages your reputation in a segment that is probably smaller than you think.

Use this verbatim consent script at the start of the interview, before any substantive questions:

```
Thanks for taking the time to participate in our study. Just so you know, the information shared will only be used for internal research purposes. With that, do you mind if I record this session?
```

If they decline recording, take notes manually. Do not push back on the decision.

## Running the interview

**Duration.** 15 to 60 minutes per interview. 30 minutes is a good default for a first round. Long enough to get depth, short enough that busy people will agree to it. Do not book 60-minute slots if 30 will do.

**Buffer.** Allow 15 to 30 minutes between interviews for notes. Running five interviews back-to-back produces five sets of mixed-up memories. The buffer is not optional.

**One question at a time.** Ask the question, then stop talking. Let silence work. Many interviewers rush to fill silence with a follow-up prompt that answers the question for the respondent. Silence means the person is thinking. Wait.

**Dig into every answer.** After each substantive response, ask why or ask for an example. Do not accept the first layer. "We find it frustrating" is a surface answer. "Why is it frustrating?" and "Can you give me a specific example?" get you beneath it.

**Do not lead.** If the respondent pauses and says "I suppose the issue is..." do not help them finish the sentence. They are working out what they actually think. Let them finish without steering.

**Body language (optional).** Expert interviewers note when the respondent leans forward, hesitates, or becomes more animated. This is not essential for a first round. Focus on asking the right questions and digging into answers before you add the layer of non-verbal observation.

## Post-interview

**Write up immediately while the interview is fresh.** Capture main impressions and key quotes within 30 minutes of ending. Do not batch five interviews and synthesise at the weekend: your memories will have merged and you will have lost the texture.

**Do not treat the first script as final.** After interview 1, you will have found gaps (things you did not ask that came up anyway) and confusions (questions that were interpreted differently than you expected). Update the script before interview 2.

**After 10 interviews, run an Affinity Mapping exercise.** Print or write out individual quotes and observations on cards or sticky notes. Cluster them by theme. Let the clusters emerge from the data rather than imposing your hypothesis on them. This is where the real patterns become visible.

## Synthesis

**Affinity Mapping.** Cluster insights around themes, ideas, and concepts. The goal is to find where multiple independent respondents landed on the same thing without being led there.

**Empathy Map.** For deep understanding of an individual persona. Captures what they think, feel, say, do, see, and hear. Useful when you need to build intuition for a specific archetype before designing for them.

**Persona Canvas.** Structured output of who the customer is. More formal than the Empathy Map. Use it to document the segment for the team.

**Update the Business Model Canvas and Value Proposition Canvas with insights.** Discovery is only useful if it flows back into the strategy artefacts. After synthesis, update the canvas with what you now know about jobs, pains, and gains. Replace assumptions with evidence.

## Evidence expectations

Discovery interviews produce qualitative evidence. They are moderate-strength for understanding jobs, pains, and gains: 10 to 20 well-run interviews with the right segment typically gets you 80% accuracy on the top three themes. They are weak evidence for buying intent. Hearing "I would pay for this" from 15 people is not the same as 15 people paying.

To generate strong evidence, pair interviews with behavioural tests:
- A concierge MVP (doing the service manually for someone who has agreed to pay)
- A landing page with a real commitment (email signup, waitlist with context, a pricing page people land on)
- A payment request (asking someone to put money down, even nominally)

These are not instead of interviews. They are the next layer. Interviews tell you what to test; behavioural tests tell you whether the thing you found is real.
