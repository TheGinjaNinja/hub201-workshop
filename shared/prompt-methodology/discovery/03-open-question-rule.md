# The Open Question Rule

## The rule

Use why, what, and how. Never do, would, could, or should.

The purpose is to surface real experience, not test opinions on hypotheticals. Closed questions (yes/no answers) and leading questions (ones that telegraph the desired answer) both produce weak evidence. They let the respondent agree with you, which costs them nothing and tells you nothing.

Open behavioural questions require the respondent to recall something that actually happened. That recall is either there or it is not. If someone cannot give you a specific example in response to "walk me through the last time you dealt with this," that absence is itself a signal.

The rule has one exception: clarifying questions mid-interview can be closed. "So this happens every month?" is fine when you are confirming a detail someone just shared. The rule applies to the primary questions in your script.

## Rewrite examples

| Closed or Leading | Open Behavioural |
|---|---|
| "Would you use this?" | "When did you last solve this, and how?" |
| "Do you think this is a problem?" | "Walk me through the last time this came up for you." |
| "Could you see yourself paying for this?" | "What did you spend on similar tools in the last 12 months?" |
| "Should we include feature X?" | "Show me how you handle this today." |
| "Is this interesting?" | "What are you spending most of your time on this quarter?" |

Each rewrite moves the question from the respondent's imagination to their recent history. The closed version asks them to project; the open version asks them to report.

Notice that none of the open versions mention the product. The moment you introduce your solution into a discovery question, you turn it into a sales question. The respondent stops reporting their reality and starts evaluating your pitch. Keep the product out of the question entirely.

## What the coach does with bad questions

When a founder drafts a script with closed or leading questions, the coach does three things: flags the pattern, shows the rewrite, and explains why the behavioural version produces stronger evidence. The coach never refuses to help with the question or blocks progress.

The explanation is brief. "This asks them to imagine using something that doesn't exist yet, which produces a yes-bias. The rewrite asks what they actually did last time, which anchors the answer in real experience." That is enough. Then move on to the next question.

If a script is heavy with leading questions, that is usually a sign the founder is more anxious about validation than learning. The coach acknowledges that impulse (it is normal) and redirects: the best way to validate is to get strong qualitative evidence first, which requires questions that do not telegraph the answer.
