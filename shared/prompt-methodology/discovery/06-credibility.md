# Credibility and Social Proof

High-status buyers will not meet a startup that looks amateur. Credibility is a prerequisite for discovery, not a marketing afterthought. This lesson comes from NPL Markets and is not in the original interview guide, but it is load-bearing for any B2B discovery programme that targets sophisticated buyers: institutional investors, procurement teams, regulated entities, senior executives at large organisations.

The mistake is to treat credibility as something you build after product-market fit. The people who can give you the best discovery data are often the ones who will ignore you if you look like a side project.

## The pre-outreach checklist

Before sending a single message to a high-status target, verify these are in place:

- A reasonable website. This does not need to be polished. It needs to exist and convey: who you are, what you are doing, and who to contact. A single well-designed page is enough. No website is disqualifying for senior buyers who Google you before accepting a meeting.
- A proper LinkedIn profile for each founder. Real photo, clear current title, recent activity (a post, an article, a comment in the last 30 days). A sparse or outdated profile signals that the person is not active in the industry.
- At least one piece of social proof. A named advisor (with their permission and their logo), a press mention however small, an event appearance, or an association with an industry body. The specific form matters less than the existence: it signals that at least one external party has assessed you and found you worth connecting with.

None of these are high-effort items for a solo founder. All three can be done in a week. They are not optional for the segment of buyers who will Google you before replying.

## The NPL Markets example

NPL Markets conducted over 100 customer and expert conversations through a combination of cold call, LinkedIn, email, and advisory network introductions. The volume was possible because the credibility infrastructure was built in parallel with the discovery process.

The CTO joined ESMA's Working Group on Non-Performing Loans and contributed to industry papers and market data standards. This was not a marketing activity. It was genuine participation in the regulatory and technical conversation that the target customers (hedge funds, servicers, banks) were already engaged in. The co-authorship of market research with industry bodies meant that when the team reached out to a potential interview candidate, they were not an unknown startup cold-calling. They were contributors to documents that the target had read or heard about.

The NPL Markets programme illustrates the compounding effect: each piece of credibility made the next conversation easier to get. The working group membership opened introductions to bank representatives. The bank introductions produced co-authored research. The co-authored research produced inbound interest from firms that would otherwise have been unreachable. By the time the product was ready to pilot, the discovery process had already built a relationship network inside the segment.

## How to manufacture credibility when you have none

Most founders in an accelerator cohort have thin credibility at the start. That is normal. The question is how to close the gap quickly before discovery outreach stalls.

Find an advisor from the cohort or your wider network whose name and background your target buyers would recognise. Agree how they want to be listed. Their logo or name next to yours on a website or in an email signature signals vetting by someone the buyer already knows. This is the fastest credibility lever available.

Speak at an event, even a small one. A panel at a local industry meetup, a guest session at a university programme, a webinar with 30 attendees: all of these are discoverable proof that someone trusted you with a public slot. List the speaking credit on your LinkedIn and website.

Get quoted in a publication, even a small industry newsletter. Reaching out to a journalist or editor to offer a comment on a trend they have written about is a quick way to get a named credit that is externally verifiable.

Publish a piece of market research. The act of producing research is itself discovery: you interview experts, compile their views, and publish the output. The experts you interviewed are now associates who will often share the piece. The piece becomes a credibility asset you reference in outreach. "We published a brief on X and would love to discuss our findings with someone at your level" is a much warmer opening than a cold ask.

Co-author with an industry body. Trade associations, regulatory bodies, and standards groups often welcome input from practitioners and researchers. Even a named contributor credit on a report that the body publishes is enough to warm up cold emails within that body's membership.

## First-meeting posture for high-status buyers

With a peer or a lower-status buyer (a founder at your stage, someone who reached out to you, a warm referral from a mutual contact), you can open a meeting with discovery questions immediately. They expect it and the power dynamic is roughly equal.

With a high-status buyer, the dynamic is different. They have agreed to the meeting partly because they expect you to have something to say. Opening with "tell me about your problems" signals that you have not done your homework and that you are there to extract, not to contribute.

The right posture for a first meeting with a senior buyer is to present yourself and your concept in the first half of the meeting. Give them context: who you are, what you have built or are building, what your working hypothesis is. This is not a pitch; it is a professional positioning statement. Then, having earned some standing in the room, shift to the discovery questions in the second half.

This is not a rule violation. You are still running a discovery meeting. You are just sequencing it to match the social expectations of the buyer you are sitting in front of.

## What the coach checks

When a founder presents a list of target companies or contacts for discovery outreach, the coach reviews whether the credibility baseline is in place before the team starts sending messages.

If the website is missing, flag it and help the founder draft a minimal one-page brief they can hand off to a developer or build in a no-code tool this week.

If the LinkedIn profiles are sparse or dated, flag the specific gaps: missing photo, unclear title, no recent activity.

If there is no social proof, help the founder identify the fastest available option: finding an advisor who is willing to be named publicly, or booking a speaking slot at an upcoming event.

The coach never blocks outreach entirely. A team with thin credibility should start sending messages immediately, but should start with the lowest-status targets while the credibility gaps are being closed. The lowest-status targets are peers, fellow founders, friends-of-friends: people who will take the meeting without Googling first. Use those conversations to sharpen the script while the credibility infrastructure catches up.
