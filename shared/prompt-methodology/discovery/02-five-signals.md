# The Five Signals

Open questions are the tool. These five signals are the purpose.

Every interview produces a mass of words. Most of it is context or noise. The five signals below are the specific things a founder is listening for across every question. A good interview script is balanced when every question elicits at least one signal and all five signals are covered across the script. If you finish an interview and you have not heard anything on one of the five, you missed something important.

## Recency and frequency

**What the signal is.** How recently the problem occurred and how often it recurs. High frequency recent problems are urgent. Low frequency distant problems are weak candidates for a product.

**Question patterns that elicit it.** "When did you last deal with this?" "How often does this come up?" "When was the most recent time you had to handle X?"

**Strong response.** A specific recent event with concrete details: "Last week we were trying to close our monthly reporting and the system wouldn't pull data from the new ERP we switched to in January." That has recency (last week), frequency implication (monthly), and context (a system change that created a new pain).

**Weak response.** "I do it sometimes." "Occasionally." "It's one of those things that comes up now and then." Abstract, unanchored in time. Follow up with "can you give me a specific recent example?" If they still cannot, the problem may not be live enough to build on.

## Concrete behaviour

**What the signal is.** A step-by-step account of what the person actually did, with real names, tools, dates, and outcomes. This is the strongest signal in discovery because it is hardest to fabricate.

**Question patterns that elicit it.** "Give me a specific example." "Walk me through exactly what happened." "Show me how you handle this today." "What did you actually do the last time this came up?"

**Strong response.** Step-by-step with specifics: "We export from Salesforce into a Google Sheet, Jamie in finance colour-codes the rows, then we email it to the partner team who paste it into their own format before putting it into the board deck. The whole process takes about four hours every month." That is concrete. You can build a product spec from it.

**Weak response.** Abstract hypothetical: "I suppose we'd probably look at the data and then decide what to do next." No names, no tools, no steps. The person is describing what seems reasonable, not what they actually do. This tells you nothing about the real workflow.

## Time and money spent

**What the signal is.** How much the person or their organisation currently invests in this problem area. Time spent is a proxy for pain severity. Money spent is the strongest signal for commercial viability because it shows an existing budget.

**Question patterns that elicit it.** "What are you spending time on in this area?" "Where does your budget go?" "What did you spend on tools in the last 12 months?" "What does this currently cost you?"

**Strong response.** Specific hours, pounds, or vendor names: "We pay about £3,000 a year for the legacy system that sort of does this, plus probably two days a month of my analyst's time on manual reconciliation." That is a budget and a cost-of-status-quo you can work with.

**Weak response.** "A lot." "It's significant." "We don't track it." No specificity. Follow up: "Can you estimate? Even a rough number is helpful." If they genuinely cannot estimate, that signals the problem may not be painful enough to have attracted budget attention.

## Problem-area match

**What the signal is.** Whether the person operates in your problem space daily, or whether they are in adjacent territory. This is a segmentation signal. The best interview candidates live inside your problem space by default; their job is the problem you are solving. Adjacent candidates have the problem occasionally or obliquely. Mismatched candidates do not have the problem at all.

**Question patterns that elicit it.** "Is what you're describing in [your space] or does it affect other parts of the business?" "Who in the organisation is most affected by this?" "Is this a daily part of your role or something that comes up at certain times?"

**Strong response.** Confirms the person is squarely in the problem space: "This is the core of what I do. I spend probably 60% of my week on this." Clear match.

**Weak response.** "Yes, we deal with that, but it's more my colleague Sarah's area." That is an adjacent signal. The interview is still useful context but the person is not your primary segment. Sarah is.

## Awareness level

**What the signal is.** Whether the respondent knows they have this problem, is aware solutions might exist, or is actively looking. This is a market-maturity signal. It affects how you sell and how you message.

Levels of awareness:
- Active: "I've been looking at tools for this, we have budget, we want to decide by Q3."
- Passive: "I know it's a problem but we haven't prioritised it yet."
- Unaware: "Hmm, I've never really thought about it as a problem. It's just how we do things."
- Denial: "It's fine. We handle it."

**Question patterns that elicit it.** "Have you looked at solutions for this?" "Is this something your business is actively trying to fix?" "What do you see as the biggest opportunity in this space right now?"

**Strong response.** Any signal about awareness level is useful. Knowing most of your segment is unaware tells you the market needs education before it needs a product. Knowing they are all actively shopping tells you distribution speed matters.

**Weak response.** There is no truly weak response here. Denial is a useful signal. Unawareness is a useful signal. What is weak is not asking at all.

## Applying the signals to script design

After writing a discovery script, tag each question with the signal(s) it elicits. Then review the distribution.

A script heavy on concrete behaviour but light on awareness level will map the workflow well but miss the market-maturity picture. A script heavy on time and money but light on problem-area match will find easy-to-sell candidates but miss the segmentation work needed to find more of them.

The goal is at least one strong question per signal across the script. In practice, recency/frequency and concrete behaviour questions should dominate (they produce the richest qualitative data). Problem-area match and awareness level questions can be lighter but should not be absent. Time and money questions feel awkward to many founders but they are load-bearing: skip them and you have no commercial baseline.
