# The Say-Do Gap

People lie to be polite. Not maliciously, but structurally: when someone asks you about their idea, you want to encourage them. You say "yes, I'd use that" and you mean it in the moment. But you never actually buy, sign up, or change your behaviour. The gap between what people say and what they do is the single biggest trap in customer discovery.

## Why it matters for discovery

Behaviour is truth. Opinions are noise.

An interview that collects opinions ("would you use this?", "do you think this is a problem?") produces weak evidence at best. The respondent is telling you what seems reasonable or polite, filtered through their desire not to disappoint you. You cannot build a product on that.

An interview that surfaces real past behaviour ("when did you last do this?", "what did you actually spend?", "walk me through what happened") produces moderate-to-strong qualitative evidence. It anchors the conversation in what the person has already done, not what they imagine they might do. The further from hypothetical you can get, the more reliable the signal.

Discovery interviews are not surveys. They are archaeology. You are digging up what already happened, not forecasting what might.

## How to close the gap in interviews

Ask about past behaviour, not future intent. "When did you last deal with this?" beats "would you deal with this?" every time.

Ask for specific examples. Push past generalities. "I deal with this all the time" is not evidence. "It happened last Tuesday when we were trying to reconcile a vendor payment and our finance system was three weeks out of date" is evidence.

Observe rather than ask hypothetically. If you can put something in front of them and watch what they do, that tells you more than ten minutes of questions about what they might do.

Pair interviews with behavioural tests where possible. A landing page that asks for an email, a concierge pilot that asks for commitment, a pricing page that asks for a card number: these produce strong evidence because the respondent has to act, not just opine. Interviews alone are not enough to validate anything commercial.

## Classic failures

The classic failure is the "would you use this?" question. It produces a yes-bias that evaporates the moment a real buying decision arrives. Respondents who said yes enthusiastically disappear when asked to commit time, money, or political capital.

The Bondcube lesson is worth preserving here. Over 290 letters of intent were collected from institutional counterparties who said they wanted to trade on an electronic platform. The evidence looked overwhelming. But the letters of intent had no behavioural evidence underneath them. No one had changed their routing. No one had committed execution volume. The polite enthusiasm of 290 conversations masked the absence of any actual behavioural change. The product launched into a gap between what buyers said and what they were prepared to do.

If an LoI, a verbal commitment, or a positive interview does not require the respondent to do anything differently, it is not evidence. It is politeness.
