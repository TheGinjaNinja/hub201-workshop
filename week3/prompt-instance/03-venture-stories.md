# Three Venture Stories

Jamie Reynolds has founded three venture-backed startups and raised over EUR 10 million across them. These three stories anchor Week 3 because each one teaches a different lesson about customer discovery. Bondcube shows what failure looks like. NPL Markets shows B2B discovery done right with high-status buyers. P.S. Bridal shows B2C discovery done right when customers will not give you their time.

## Bondcube -- the warning

Bondcube was an institutional B2B fixed income bond trading platform. The buyers were asset managers, hedge funds, and banks.

Jamie personally signed 40 Letters of Intent. The team reached 250 LoIs with investment institutions in total. Every meeting felt good. Everyone was polite, enthusiastic, interested. The team thought they were heroes.

They never collected any meaningful feedback. They asked leading questions. They took polite enthusiasm as a buying signal. They did not probe for behaviour, they did not follow up to see whether the intent turned into action, and they did not build their discovery around real experiences the institutions had already had with adjacent problems.

They were unknowingly setting themselves up for failure.

**Lessons:**
- Quicksand of No Validation: 250 LoIs is not 250 behavioural data points. It is 250 polite yeses.
- Ego Echo-Chamber: when everyone in the room agrees with you, you are not learning. You are being entertained.
- LoIs without behavioural evidence are a vanity metric.
- Polite enthusiasm is the weakest signal on the Evidence Strength Scale. Rating it as anything stronger than "weak" is a failure of discipline.

## NPL Markets -- B2B done right

NPL Markets was an NPL (non-performing loan) trading platform. The buyers were hedge funds and banks. These buyers are high-status and busy. They do not meet unknown startups.

The team ran more than 100 customer and expert conversations to map the market, the early-customer profiles, and the ideal customer profile. The sourcing channels were cold call, LinkedIn, personalised email, and warm intros through their advisory networks.

First meetings were run with a specific posture. For the first half, the team presented themselves and the concept. The sales posture earned the right to ask discovery questions in the second half. For high-status buyers, this sequencing is essential. Opening a cold first meeting with "tell me about your problems" does not work when the buyer has no reason to trust you.

Between first meetings, the team built a social-proof engine. They went to industry events. They ran expert interviews that turned into published market research reports. The CTO joined ESMA's Working Group on Non-Performing Loans and contributed to industry papers and market data standards. They partnered with the regulator to co-author papers.

All of this made the next round of cold outreach land. When a hedge fund portfolio manager saw a cold email referencing an ESMA paper the team had co-authored, the meeting happened.

Only after the market was mapped did the team build a concierge-style MVP that tested the actual need with real transactions. They found Customer/Problem Fit, then Problem/Solution Fit, then started on Revenue Model Fit.

**Lessons:**
- Credibility buys the meeting with high-status B2B buyers. Website, LinkedIn, social proof are discovery prerequisites, not marketing afterthoughts.
- First meetings with sophisticated buyers are credibility-first, discovery-second. Do not invert the sequence.
- Build social proof as a by-product of discovery (expert interviews produce research reports, which produce credibility).
- Concierge MVP lets you validate the need without building a platform. You do the work manually for early customers before you automate.

## P.S. Bridal -- B2C done right

P.S. Bridal was a B2C personalised wedding dress styling business. The buyers were brides. Brides will not schedule interviews with strangers.

The team could not use classical customer interviews. So they gave value first. They built a wedding dress style finder as a prototype -- brides answered a 30-question Typeform, the team ran a manual algorithm on the responses, and brides received a personalised style recommendation. In exchange, brides shared preferences, wedding venue details, dress size, budget, jewellery, services needed, and timing. The team captured 40+ data points per bride.

2,500 brides went through the style finder in the first year. 8,000+ over time. Every interaction was discovery disguised as value delivery.

After the style finder, the team added a WhatsApp concierge for direct advice. Then a booking prototype to connect brides with boutiques. Then a style pack tool. Then a directory.

Each prototype taught the team something specific. The booking prototype told them brides struggled to find boutiques that stocked their ideal dress. The team confirmed this with real data ("67% of brides find it hard") and built a booking product around it. Over 100 brides booked through the booking tool each month at a 76% conversion rate.

Two years of part-time work later, the team had a strong value proposition. They also realised the business model needed to change from sale to rental. That pivot came out of the data the prototypes had been collecting all along.

**Lessons:**
- In B2C, the prototype is the interview. You earn the conversation by giving value first.
- A value-giving prototype delivers discovery insights at scale that interviews never can.
- Iterative prototype ladders compound learning. Each prototype validates or invalidates the last.
- Business model fit sometimes comes after value proposition fit, and data from prototypes is what tells you when to pivot.

## The common arc

NPL Markets and P.S. Bridal went through completely different discovery motions (sales-style first meetings vs. value-giving prototypes), but they reached the same two waypoints: Customer/Problem Fit then Problem/Solution Fit.

The shared discipline across both:
- A narrow target (not "hedge funds" or "brides" -- specific sub-segments with specific triggers)
- Obsession with behaviour over opinion (real transactions, real usage data, real spend, not survey answers)
- Iteration (scripts updated after every interview, prototypes rebuilt after every learning)
- Social proof built into the process (ESMA papers for NPL, 8,000 bride data points for P.S. Bridal)

Bondcube had none of these. 250 LoIs, no narrowing, no behavioural probes, no iteration, no external credibility flywheel. Which story is playing out in your team is the single biggest question this week.
