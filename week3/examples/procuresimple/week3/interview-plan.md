## Target Segment

Head of Procurement (or Chief Procurement Officer, Procurement Director, Procurement Manager) at a 500-2000 person European manufacturer in metals, machinery, or food processing that has acquired or merged with another company in the last 18 months.

**Why this trigger:** Post-merger manufacturers have inherited fragmented supplier bases, mismatched procurement processes, and pressure to rationalise spend. This makes the admin burden concrete and urgent rather than chronic and tolerated. They are also more likely to be actively evaluating new tools. Two team members should independently find the same person on LinkedIn using: title + company size + European manufacturing vertical + acquisition news trigger.

---

## Consent Script

Use this verbatim at the start of every interview, before any substantive questions:

> "Thanks for taking the time to participate in our study. Just so you know, the information shared will only be used for internal research purposes. With that, do you mind if I record this session?"

If they decline recording, take notes manually. Do not push back. A declined recording is not a failed interview. Ask if they are comfortable with written notes being kept.

## Interview Script

Length guidance: 30-45 minutes. Book 45-minute slots and end early if natural. Do not rush to fit all questions in. The most important questions are in Business and Personal. If time runs short, drop Industry questions, not Business ones.

Each question is tagged with [Hx] for the hypothesis it tests (from hypotheses.md) and [signal] for the Five Signal it elicits.

### Intro (4 questions)

1. "What's your job title and how long have you been in the role?"
   [context]
   *Note: establishes seniority and tenure. Longer tenure means more institutional memory of how procurement has worked across different tools and processes.*

2. "What was your role before this one?"
   [context]
   *Note: surfaces domain depth and prior tool exposure. Someone who came from a large enterprise may have different expectations about tooling than someone who grew up in SME environments.*

3. "How big is your procurement team, and how is it structured?"
   [H2 | concrete behaviour]
   *Note: reveals whether there is a CPO above them, whether procurement has its own budget, and whether they have enough headcount that admin automation would free up meaningful capacity.*

4. "Walk me through what a typical Monday morning looks like for you."
   [H1 | time and money spent]
   *Note: open question that lets them volunteer what takes their attention before you direct the conversation. If supplier chasing comes up unprompted, that is a strong problem-area match signal.*

---

### Industry (3 questions)

5. "What's happening in procurement in your sector right now? Where are you hearing about it?"
   [signal: awareness level]
   *Note: surfaces market maturity, where procurement leads get information, and whether digital transformation of procurement is actively discussed in their peer network.*

6. "How has procurement complexity changed over the last two years in your vertical?"
   [signal: recency and frequency]
   *Note: reveals whether the problem is getting worse (urgency signal) or stable. Post-merger manufacturers will often flag a step-change in complexity.*

7. "What's the biggest challenge facing procurement teams your size right now?"
   [signal: problem-area match]
   *Note: open industry question. Do not steer toward supplier admin or invoices. Let them name the problem category. If they name cost reduction, budget ownership becomes more important. If they name admin burden, H1 is live.*

---

### Business (12 questions)

8. "When did you last onboard a new supplier? Walk me through the first week."
   [H1 | concrete behaviour]
   *Note: the first high-value behavioural question. A detailed account of a recent supplier onboarding reveals exactly which steps are manual, who is involved, and where friction lives. Push for specifics: "Who sent the first email? Who set up the payment terms? Who entered them into the system?"*

9. "What's the last invoice dispute your team had with a supplier? Walk me through how it got resolved."
   [H1 | concrete behaviour]
   *Note: invoice reconciliation is one of the two core time drains in the hypothesis. A real dispute story surfaces the process, the tools used, how long it took, and who was involved.*

10. "Who signed off the last software purchase your team made? What was the process?"
    [H2 | concrete behaviour]
    *Note: the single most important business question. Ask it plainly. Most people will answer it. If they hesitate, follow up with: "Is there a threshold below which you can sign off independently?" The answer to H2 lives here.*

11. "What's the biggest software tool your team has bought in the last 18 months? Walk me through that evaluation."
    [H2 + H3 | recency and frequency]
    *Note: surfaces the full procurement journey for a real purchase: who initiated it, who approved it, how long it took, and what the contract structure was. Annual or monthly tells you about H3 directly.*

12. "Have you ever tried to use your ERP's procurement module? Walk me through it."
    [H4 | concrete behaviour]
    *Note: do not describe the problem. Let them describe whether it exists. A "yes, it works fine" is a direct challenge to H4 and the displacement story. A "yes, we tried it and gave up" validates H4.*

13. "Why did that stick or not stick with the team?"
    [H4 | concrete behaviour]
    *Note: follow-on from Q12. Listen for the specific reason: complexity, IT dependency, workflow mismatch, training burden, or something else. The reason shapes both the product and the sales pitch.*

14. "Can you show me the last month's supplier spend? How is it categorised?"
    [H5 | concrete behaviour]
    *Note: a direct data-sharing probe. If they pull up a spreadsheet or a live report without hesitation, data sharing is a low barrier. If they hesitate, ask: "What would it take for you to share that with a vendor in an evaluation?" This surfaces H5 directly.*

15. "When did your company last acquire or merge with another business? What changed for procurement?"
    [signal: recency and frequency]
    *Note: the ICP trigger question. If the answer is "we merged 14 months ago and have two supplier databases that still are not reconciled," that is a live, urgent version of the admin burden problem.*

16. "How many active suppliers does your team manage? How many were there 18 months ago?"
    [H1 | time and money spent]
    *Note: growth in supplier count without growth in procurement headcount is a direct proxy for admin burden. The delta between 18 months ago and today tells you whether the problem is accelerating.*

17. "What does your team do when a supplier invoice does not match the purchase order? Walk me through the last time that happened."
    [H1 | concrete behaviour]
    *Note: PO-invoice mismatch handling is one of the most time-intensive manual tasks in procurement. A real example reveals whether the process is ad hoc (high admin time) or systematised (lower admin time).*

18. "What tools does your team currently use for supplier communication, PO management, and invoice approval? Take me through the stack."
    [H4 | problem-area match]
    *Note: reveals the current tool landscape. A mix of ERP, email, spreadsheets, and phone is the baseline the team is assuming. If they have a modern stack already (e.g. Coupa or Jaggaer), the displacement story changes.*

19. "What's the last process improvement project your procurement team actually completed? What made it stick?"
    [H4 | concrete behaviour]
    *Note: surfaces what makes an operational change succeed in this environment. Useful for understanding what implementation support they would need and what their tolerance for change management effort is.*

---

### Personal (6 questions)

20. "What's taking up most of your team's time this quarter?"
    [H1 | time and money spent]
    *Note: if supplier admin comes up unprompted, that is a strong problem-area match signal. If it does not come up at all, probe: "Where does supplier and invoice management sit in that picture?"*

21. "How many invoices does your team reconcile per month? How long does that take roughly?"
    [H1 | time and money spent]
    *Note: push for a rough number. "I'm not sure" is acceptable; "maybe 300 invoices a month and it takes 2-3 days to clear the backlog" is a data point you can use to build an ROI case.*

22. "What part of supplier management is most manual for your team right now?"
    [H1 | problem-area match]
    *Note: open invitation to name the pain in their own words. Do not suggest options. The answer tells you what framing resonates and what product language will land.*

23. "Have you had a supplier crisis in the last 12 months? Walk me through it."
    [signal: recency and frequency]
    *Note: a crisis story reveals the downstream consequences of poor supplier visibility. Cost overruns, production delays, and board escalations all emerge from supplier management failures. Connects admin burden to commercial impact.*

24. "What data or metrics does your team track about procurement performance? What do you share upward?"
    [H1 | concrete behaviour]
    *Note: reveals whether procurement time is already measured. If they track cycle times, cost savings, or supplier performance, ask what the numbers are. If they do not track it, note that as a signal about problem awareness.*

25. "If you could remove one thing from your week, what would it be?"
    [signal: awareness level]
    *Note: the most unguarded question in the personal section. Ask it after 25-30 minutes of conversation when defences are lower. The answer at this point is often less filtered than anything earlier in the conversation.*

---

### Outro (3 questions)

26. "If you had a magic wand for your procurement team on Monday morning, what one thing would you fix?"
    [signal: awareness level]
    *Note: the classic magic wand close. Ask it last, after the full conversation. The answer here is often more specific and honest than earlier responses.*

27. "Is there anyone else in your network I should be talking to about this? Someone who handles procurement differently, or at a different size manufacturer?"
    [signal: referral]
    *Note: ask for a specific name, not a vague yes. "Who is the first person that comes to mind?" Manufacturing is relationship-driven. A warm intro from this interviewee is more valuable than five cold outreach messages.*

28. "What's the one question I should have asked you that I didn't?"
    [signal: awareness level]
    *Note: the closing meta-question. Surfaces blind spots in the script. If the same gap appears twice across interviews, update the script before the next interview.*

## Finance Director Variant

If interviewing a Finance Director or CFO at a target manufacturer (to test H2 directly), replace Business questions 8-9 and 14-19 with these:

- "What does your procurement team spend on software today? How did that decision get made?"
  [H2 | time and money spent]

- "When did you last approve a software purchase for a department other than Finance? Walk me through the process."
  [H2 + H3 | concrete behaviour]

- "How visible is your procurement spend to you as Finance Director? What does the reporting look like?"
  [H1 | concrete behaviour]

- "If a Procurement Head brought you a tool proposal for EUR 30,000 per year, what would you need to see to approve it?"
  [H2 + H3 | concrete behaviour]

- "What's the biggest area of spend in your business where you feel you have the least visibility or control?"
  [signal: problem-area match]

## Red-Flag Question Patterns to Avoid

These question forms are banned from the script. They generate politeness bias, not evidence.

| Banned pattern | Why it fails | Rewrite |
|---|---|---|
| "Would you use a tool like this?" | Hypothetical approval. People say yes to be polite. | "How does your team handle this today?" |
| "Don't you find supplier chasing really time-consuming?" | Leading question. You have answered it for them. | "Walk me through the last time you chased a supplier for a missing invoice." |
| "How important is spend visibility to you?" | Importance question gets an importance answer. Useless. | "What did you do the last time you needed to produce a spend breakdown for the board?" |
| "Would you pay for this if it existed?" | Pure hypothetical. Means nothing. | "What did your team spend on operational software in the last 12 months? Where did it go?" |
| "Is procurement a big problem at your company?" | Yes/no, almost always yes. Tells you nothing actionable. | "When did you last onboard a new supplier? Walk me through the first week." |

## Cadence Plan

Manufacturing procurement leads are harder to reach than fintech security leads. Expect lower LinkedIn response rates and longer lead times to a booked call. Build in a second-touch follow-up plan from the start.

| Week | Target | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| Week 1 | 5 interviews (mix of procurement leads and finance leads) | Run interviews 1-5. Update script after interview 1. Focus on H1 and H2. |
| Week 2 | 5 interviews | Run interviews 6-10. Note emerging patterns. Adjust Finance Director variant if needed. |
| After interview 10 | Affinity mapping session (3 hours) | Cluster insights by theme. Decide whether H2 flips the GTM before continuing. |
| Week 3 | 5 interviews (adjusted segment or adjusted questions) | Run interviews 11-15 with refined script. |
| Week 4 | 5 interviews | Run interviews 16-20. Begin synthesis. |
| Before Week 4 workshop | 20 interviews completed | Arrive with full synthesis, updated hypotheses, and at least 3 named potential buyers. |

**Script review points:** After interview 1 (catch misunderstood questions), after interview 5 (first-round synthesis), after interview 10 (major affinity map, possible full script rebuild if H2 is validated and the buyer shifts to Finance).

**Second-touch follow-up plan for unresponsive contacts:** Manufacturing buyers are more relationship-driven than fintech. Cold outreach alone will not get to 20 interviews. Follow this protocol for every unresponsive contact:

- Day 1: Send first message (LinkedIn or email) referencing the specific trigger.
- Day 7: Send a second-touch message from a different angle. Example: if the first message referenced the acquisition, the second message references a specific industry challenge ("I have been speaking to heads of procurement across metals manufacturers about how they handle supplier consolidation after a merger. Would a 20-minute conversation be useful?").
- Day 14: If no response after two messages, move on. Do not send a third message to the same person. Move them to a "revisit in 60 days" column.

**Interview target total:** 20 before Week 4. Minimum 10 before drawing conclusions about H1 and H2.
