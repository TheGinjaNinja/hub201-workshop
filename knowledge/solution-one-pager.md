# Solution One-Pager: Coaching Guide

## Purpose

Guide the founder to create a customer-facing one-pager that communicates value in terms of outcomes, not features. This is the document a founder sends after a first meeting, shares at a conference, or leaves behind with a potential buyer. It is not an investor document. No TAM slides, no team bios, no funding history.

## Key Principle: Customer Outcomes First

The single most common mistake founders make on a one-pager is leading with technology. "We use machine learning to..." is about you. "You reduce breach response time from 4 hours to 15 minutes" is about the customer. Every sentence on this document should pass the test: does this tell the reader what changes for them?

### The ROI Ladder

Push every feature claim upward through the ROI Ladder. Start with the capability, then chain through "which means..." until you reach a business outcome the buyer cares about.

Example chain for a cybersecurity startup:
- **Feature:** Automated log correlation across cloud environments
- **Which means...** your security team spends less time triaging false positives
- **Which means...** analysts focus on genuine threats instead of noise
- **Which means...** mean time to detect drops from days to hours
- **Which means...** you reduce the cost and blast radius of a breach
- **Outcome:** Lower incident costs, fewer compliance penalties, reduced cyber insurance premiums

The one-pager should land at the top of this ladder. Features appear only in the "How It Works" section, framed as mechanisms that deliver the outcome.

### Think Like the Customer

Customers buy products (features, specs, components) but what they want is the outcome. A CISO does not want a SIEM. A CISO wants confidence that threats are detected before they become incidents. Write for what the customer wants, not what you built.

### Be Specific Enough to Test

From the Commercial Offer Canvas: "competitive pricing" is not an offer. "£500/month for up to 10 users" is an offer. The same applies here. "We help companies improve security" is not a one-pager. "We cut SOC alert triage time by 60% for mid-market financial services firms" is a one-pager worth reading.

If the founder cannot be specific, they have not done enough customer discovery. That is useful information. Flag it and help them identify what they need to learn before this document can work.

## Template Structure

Coach the founder through these six sections. Word counts are guides, not hard limits. The entire document should fit on a single page when printed or viewed as a PDF.

### 1. The Problem (40-60 words)

What is broken in the customer's world today? Who feels this pain most acutely? What is the cost of doing nothing?

Coach the founder to write this from the buyer's perspective, not their own. The problem should be recognisable to the target customer within the first sentence.

**Cybersecurity example:**
"Mid-market financial services firms face the same regulatory scrutiny as global banks but lack the security teams to match. With an average of 3 security staff managing 10,000+ daily alerts, genuine threats get buried. A missed critical alert costs an average of $150K in incident response alone, before regulators get involved."

**Watch for:**
- Problems stated as absence of the founder's product ("The problem is companies don't have our solution")
- Problems so broad they apply to everyone ("Cybersecurity is a growing concern")
- No cost of inaction stated

### 2. Who This Is For (20-30 words)

Name the specific buyer profile and the trigger event that makes them care right now. This section should let a reader self-select in or out within seconds.

**Cybersecurity example:**
"IT Security Managers at financial services firms (200-2,000 employees) preparing for NIS2 compliance deadlines or recovering from a failed audit."

**Watch for:**
- "All companies" or "any organisation" targeting
- Missing the trigger event (why now, not just who)
- Describing the user but not the buyer (the person who signs the cheque)

### 3. What Changes (40-60 words)

This is the core of the one-pager. What is different in the customer's world after they adopt this solution? Use before/after framing. Quantify wherever possible.

This section must be about the customer's changed reality, not about the product. Test: cover the startup name and this section should still make sense as a description of a better future state.

**Cybersecurity example:**
"Before: your team manually triages thousands of alerts daily, missing critical threats buried in noise. After: automated correlation reduces actionable alerts to under 50 per day, with critical threats surfaced and prioritised in under 10 minutes. Your team shifts from reactive firefighting to proactive threat hunting. First audit after deployment: zero critical findings."

**Watch for:**
- "What Changes" that is actually a feature list in disguise
- No quantification ("things get better" rather than "response time drops by 60%")
- Written about the product rather than the customer's experience

### 4. How It Works (40-60 words, 3-4 bullets)

This is the only section where features appear, and they should be framed as mechanisms that deliver the outcome described above. Use plain language. A non-technical buyer (a CFO, a compliance officer, a board member) should be able to follow this.

**Cybersecurity example:**
- Connects to your existing SIEM and cloud logs in under 2 hours. No rip-and-replace.
- Correlates alerts across sources using behavioural patterns, not static rules.
- Surfaces prioritised incidents with recommended response actions for your team.
- Generates audit-ready compliance reports mapped to NIS2 and ISO 27001 requirements.

**Watch for:**
- Technical jargon without explanation ("uses transformer-based NLP models")
- More than 4 bullets (this is a one-pager, not a datasheet)
- Features that do not connect back to the outcome in section 3

### 5. Proof (20-40 words, 2-3 points)

Customer quotes, pilot results, metrics, certifications, or notable early adopters. This is where credibility lives. If the founder has no proof yet, that is fine, but they must state what they plan to prove and how. "We plan to..." for every item is a red flag that indicates the startup is too early for a customer-facing one-pager and may need to focus on running pilots first.

**Cybersecurity examples (with proof):**
- "Alert triage time dropped 70% in our 3-month pilot with a Tier 2 bank."
- ISO 27001 certified. SOC 2 Type II in progress.
- "We went from 3 failed audit items to zero within one quarter." (Head of IT Security, mid-market insurer)

**Cybersecurity examples (without proof yet):**
- Currently running a 60-day pilot with two financial services firms. Results expected Q3 2026.
- SOC 2 Type II certification in progress, expected completion August 2026.

**Watch for:**
- No proof and no plan to get proof
- Vanity metrics ("500 sign-ups" when none are paying)
- Proof that does not relate to the outcome claimed in section 3

### 6. Next Step (10-15 words)

One clear call to action. Not two options. Not "contact us to learn more." A specific next step that moves the relationship forward.

**Good examples:**
- "Book a 30-minute technical walkthrough with a live demo of your own data."
- "Start a free 14-day pilot. Setup takes under 2 hours."
- "Request a compliance gap assessment. We will map your NIS2 readiness in one session."

**Watch for:**
- Vague CTAs: "Contact us to learn more" or "Visit our website"
- Multiple CTAs competing for attention
- CTAs that require too much commitment too early (e.g., "Sign a 12-month contract")

## Coaching Questions

Use these questions to pressure-test the draft. Ask them conversationally, not as a checklist dump.

1. **Relevance test:** If a customer reads only the first two sections (The Problem and Who This Is For), will they know immediately whether this is relevant to them?
2. **Clarity test:** Can a non-technical buyer (a CFO or compliance officer) understand the "How It Works" section without asking for explanations?
3. **Outcome test:** Is "What Changes" about your product or about what changes for the customer? Cover the product name. Does it still read as a compelling future state?
4. **Evidence test:** Do you have any proof? If not, what is the fastest path to getting some? A single pilot result is worth more than ten claims.
5. **Inbox test:** Would you respond to this if it landed in your inbox on a busy Tuesday morning? If not, what would make you open it?

## Red Flags

Flag these patterns directly. They indicate the founder needs to rethink their approach before this document will work.

- **Technology-first framing:** Opening with "We use machine learning to..." or "Our proprietary algorithm..." The customer does not care about the technology. They care about the outcome.
- **No proof anywhere:** Every claim is aspirational. "We plan to..." appears in the proof section for every point. This suggests the startup is pre-validation and should focus on running pilots before creating a customer-facing one-pager.
- **Vague CTA:** "Contact us to learn more" signals the founder has not thought about what happens after someone reads this document. A one-pager without a clear next step is a dead end.
- **One-pager that is actually three pages:** If it does not fit on one page, it is not a one-pager. Ruthless editing is required. Every word must earn its place.
- **Feature soup:** The "How It Works" section has 8 bullets covering every capability. This is a datasheet, not a one-pager. Pick the 3-4 mechanisms that directly deliver the claimed outcome.
- **No specificity:** "We help companies improve their security posture" could describe 10,000 vendors. If you cannot tell what makes this startup different from the first two sections, the positioning work is incomplete.

## Output Format

The final one-pager should be saved to `/outputs/solution-one-pager.md` with this structure:

```
# [Startup Name]
**[Tagline: one sentence that captures the core value proposition]**

## The Problem
[40-60 words]

## Who This Is For
[20-30 words]

## What Changes
[40-60 words, before/after framing]

## How It Works
- [Bullet 1]
- [Bullet 2]
- [Bullet 3]
- [Bullet 4, optional]

## Proof
- [Point 1]
- [Point 2]
- [Point 3, optional]

## Next Step
[One clear CTA, 10-15 words]
```

After the one-pager content, include:

```
---

## Evidence Assessment
[Rate the strength of proof: Strong (customer data, pilot results) / Emerging (early pilots, certifications in progress) / Weak (no external validation yet). Note what would strengthen it.]

## Coaching Notes
[Key observations from the coaching session. What did the founder struggle with? What needs more customer discovery? What is the biggest risk in this positioning?]
```

## Coaching Flow

1. **Start with the problem.** Ask the founder to describe the problem they solve in plain language, as if explaining to a friend who is not technical. Listen for whether they describe the customer's pain or their own technology.
2. **Identify the buyer.** Push for specificity. "Who exactly would buy this, and what just happened in their world that makes them care right now?"
3. **Build the outcome.** Use the ROI Ladder. Start with what the product does, then chain "which means..." until you reach a business outcome. That outcome becomes the "What Changes" section.
4. **Add the mechanism.** Only now introduce features, framed as how the outcome is delivered. Keep it to 3-4 bullets in plain language.
5. **Assess proof.** Be honest about where the founder stands. Strong proof makes the one-pager credible. No proof means the one-pager is a hypothesis, and the next step should be getting proof.
6. **Define the next step.** One action. Specific. Low enough commitment that a busy buyer would consider it.
7. **Run the coaching questions.** Pressure-test the draft against the five questions above. Iterate until the founder is satisfied and the document passes all tests.
