# Personas: ProcureSimple

**Date:** 2026-04-08
**Exercise:** Buyer and User Personas (Customer Clarity Canvas)

## Beachhead Definition

**Segment:** Manufacturing SMEs with 50-200 employees in one European country (Czech Republic), spending EUR 5M+ annually on procurement across fragmented supplier categories.

**Why this segment first:**
- Manufacturing SMEs in this size range have significant procurement spend but no dedicated procurement team. The Operations Manager handles purchasing alongside everything else.
- Rising input costs across European manufacturing have created board-level pressure to cut costs by 10%. This is a trigger that creates urgency right now.
- Companies in this range are too small to hire a procurement consultant full-time but too large to keep running everything through spreadsheets and phone calls.
- Manufacturing SMEs in the same country attend the same trade events, are members of the same industry associations, and share suppliers. Word of mouth works within this community.

**Target list size:** Approximately 80 companies. The founders can currently name 12 from discovery interviews and industry contacts. The rest can be identified through Czech Manufacturing Association membership lists and trade directories.

**Adjacent segments for expansion:**
1. Retail SMEs (50-200 employees) in the Czech Republic. Same procurement pain, different supplier categories, reachable through the same country-level business networks.
2. Manufacturing SMEs in Slovakia, Poland, and Austria. Same industry, similar company profiles, shared EU regulatory environment.
3. Larger manufacturing companies (200-500 employees) once case studies and measurable savings data are established.

## Persona Cards

### "Spreadsheet-Drowning Karel"

**Buying Role:** Champion + End User
**Early or Ideal Customer:** Early Customer

#### Define the Customer

- **Role/Title:** Operations Manager (sometimes titled Plant Manager or Head of Operations)
- **Firmographics:** Manufacturing (components, packaging, light industrial), 50-200 employees, EUR 10-30M revenue, Czech Republic. Family-owned or privately held. No dedicated procurement team.
- **Role in Purchase:** Champion and End User. Karel is the person who feels procurement pain daily. He will discover ProcureSimple, evaluate it, and push the CFO or owner to approve the spend. He is also the person who will use the tool day-to-day.
- **Trigger Event:** Rising input costs squeezing margins. The board (or the owner) has told Karel to find ways to cut procurement costs by 10% this year. He has no data, no tools, and no time to run a proper procurement project.
- **Where They Hang Out:** Czech Manufacturing Association events, regional industry trade fairs, LinkedIn (follows supply chain and operations content, posts occasionally), peer networks of operations managers at supplier meetups, local Chambers of Commerce.
- **Who Influences Them:** Industry peers at similar-sized manufacturers, trade association publications, supplier account managers (who have strong relationships), the company's external accountant or financial advisor, occasional articles in Czech business media.

#### Define the Use Case

- **Situation Before Your Product:** Karel manages procurement alongside production scheduling, logistics, quality, and supplier relationships. He does not have a procurement team. He is the procurement team. Purchasing decisions happen in his inbox, on his phone, and in spreadsheets he has built over the years.
- **Current Solution:** Excel spreadsheets for tracking orders and supplier contacts. Email and phone calls for requesting quotes and negotiating prices. An external purchasing consultant brought in once a year for the largest contracts. The company's accounting system records what was paid, but not what was bought or at what unit price.
- **Frustrations with Current Approach:** No visibility into total spend by category. Cannot tell the board where money goes without spending two days pulling data together manually. Negotiates alone with suppliers, so he has no leverage compared to larger buyers. Spends 2+ full days every month on procurement administration that adds no strategic value. Quote comparison is manual, slow, and error-prone. Supplier contracts renew automatically because nobody tracks expiry dates.
- **Consequences:** The company overpays on key categories because Karel has no benchmarking data and no leverage. Board meetings include uncomfortable questions about procurement costs that Karel cannot answer with data. Time spent on procurement admin is time not spent on production and operations, which is his actual job. Missed contract renewal dates lock the company into unfavourable terms for another year.
- **Past Spend:** Has paid a purchasing consultant EUR 5-8K for one-off projects (annual supplier renegotiation). Has not previously purchased procurement software. Assumes SaaS tools are designed for large enterprises and priced accordingly. Budget expectation for a tool would be EUR 200-500/month, but would need to see clear ROI to justify it to the owner.

#### Define the Problem

- **Jobs to Be Done:**
  1. Reduce procurement costs so the business can protect margins while input prices rise
  2. Get visibility into total supplier spend by category, supplier, and time period so he can answer board questions with data
  3. Spend less personal time on procurement administration so he can focus on production and operations (his core responsibility)
- **Pain Points (ranked by severity):**
  1. No consolidated view of supplier spend. Data is scattered across spreadsheets, emails, and the accounting system. Cannot produce a spend breakdown without days of manual work. (Critical)
  2. No negotiating leverage. Each company negotiates individually with suppliers. Karel knows he is paying more than larger buyers but has no mechanism to change this. (Critical)
  3. Spends 2+ full days per month on procurement admin: chasing quotes, comparing prices in spreadsheets, following up on deliveries, tracking contract dates. (Major)
  4. Cannot benchmark whether current supplier pricing is competitive. No market data, no peer comparisons, just gut feeling. (Minor)
- **How Often They Feel This Pain:** Weekly. Every supplier interaction, every purchase order, every board meeting where cost questions come up.
- **Problem Awareness:** High. Karel is actively looking for a way to improve procurement. He has searched for tools online but found mostly enterprise solutions priced at EUR 50K+/year. He assumes there is nothing affordable for a company his size.
- **Why They Would Say No:** "We cannot afford another SaaS tool." "I do not have time to implement something new." "Our suppliers are loyal, we do not want to damage relationships." "I have been doing this in spreadsheets for years, it works well enough." "What happens to our data if your startup goes under?"

#### Define the Buyers (B2B)

- **Champion:** Karel himself. He feels the pain, owns the problem, and will push for a solution.
- **Influencer:** The company's external accountant or financial advisor, who sees the spend data and may recommend better tools.
- **Decision Maker:** The owner/CEO at companies under 100 employees. The Finance Director at companies in the 100-200 range.
- **Budget Holder:** The owner or Finance Director. Karel does not typically control budget for new software purchases.
- **Saboteur:** IT Manager (if one exists). Concerned about data security, integration with existing systems, and supporting another tool. At smaller companies, this role may not exist, which actually removes a barrier.
- **End User:** Karel (primary), plus any admin staff who handle purchase orders or supplier communications.

#### Channels

- Czech Manufacturing Association membership directory and events (can identify and reach 30-40 operations managers directly)
- LinkedIn outreach to Operations Managers at Czech manufacturing companies in the 50-200 employee range
- Regional industry trade fairs (2-3 per year in Czech Republic)
- Referrals from pilot customers (once 2-3 are using the product)
- Content marketing targeting "procurement for SMEs" search terms in Czech and English

### "Numbers-First Elena"

**Buying Role:** Decision Maker + Budget Holder
**Early or Ideal Customer:** Ideal Customer

#### Define the Customer

- **Role/Title:** Finance Director or CFO
- **Firmographics:** Manufacturing or retail, 200-500 employees, EUR 30-80M revenue, European. Has a small finance team but no procurement function. Financial reporting is solid, but procurement data is a black hole.
- **Role in Purchase:** Decision Maker and Budget Holder. Elena approves software purchases and controls the operating budget. She does not initiate procurement tool searches, but she has the authority to approve or reject them. She would respond to a compelling cost-savings proposition.
- **Trigger Event:** Board mandate to reduce operating costs as part of the annual budget planning cycle. Elena is tasked with finding savings, and procurement is the area where she has the least data and the most suspicion that money is being wasted.
- **Where They Hang Out:** CFO peer networks and roundtables, finance conferences, LinkedIn (follows finance and operations content), industry benchmarking reports, local business press.
- **Who Influences Them:** External auditors, board members, peer CFOs at companies of similar size, management consultancies (if engaged for cost reduction projects), ERP vendor account managers.

#### Define the Use Case

- **Situation Before Your Product:** Elena's accounting system shows total spend per supplier, but nothing more. She cannot break spend down by category, compare prices across suppliers, or identify trends. She knows procurement is inefficient because she sees the total numbers, but she has no way to diagnose where the waste is.
- **Current Solution:** Accounting system (invoices recorded against supplier accounts). Occasional ad hoc analysis by asking the Operations Manager for data, which arrives days later in a messy spreadsheet. No category management, no benchmarking, no trend analysis. Annual budget planning uses last year's numbers plus a percentage adjustment.
- **Frustrations with Current Approach:** Cannot identify savings opportunities from financial data alone. The accounting system tells her what was paid, not whether the price was competitive. Relies on the Operations Manager's gut feeling for supplier negotiations, with no way to verify. When the board asks for a cost reduction plan, she has procurement data that is months old and manually compiled.
- **Consequences:** Board loses confidence that operating costs are being managed proactively. Elena approves procurement spend she cannot analyse or challenge. Cost reduction initiatives focus on headcount and overheads (where she has data) instead of procurement (where the savings may be larger but invisible). The company misses savings opportunities because nobody can see them.
- **Past Spend:** Has approved spend on ERP systems, accounting tools, and business intelligence. Would consider a procurement tool if the ROI case is clear and the price is proportional to demonstrable savings. Budget expectation: would pay EUR 500-1,000/month if the tool could prove EUR 50K+ in annual savings.

#### Define the Problem

- **Jobs to Be Done:**
  1. Identify concrete cost reduction opportunities in procurement spend so she can deliver the savings the board has asked for
  2. Get reliable procurement data (by category, supplier, and trend) so she can make informed decisions instead of relying on gut feeling
  3. Build a more professional, data-driven approach to procurement without the cost of hiring a full procurement team
- **Pain Points (ranked by severity):**
  1. Cannot identify savings opportunities from financial data alone. The accounting system shows totals, not competitiveness. She approves spend without knowing whether the company is overpaying. (Critical)
  2. Relies on the Operations Manager's gut feeling for supplier negotiations. No way to verify whether current prices are competitive. (Major)
  3. Board asks for a cost reduction plan, and she cannot produce one that includes procurement because she has no data to base it on. (Major)
- **How Often They Feel This Pain:** Monthly (when reviewing financials), quarterly (when reporting to the board), annually (during budget planning). Less frequent than Karel but higher-stakes moments.
- **Problem Awareness:** Medium. Elena knows procurement is inefficient but is not actively seeking a procurement tool. She would respond to a concrete savings opportunity ("we can show you where you are overpaying") rather than a product pitch ("here is our procurement platform").
- **Why They Would Say No:** "Show me the ROI first." "We just renewed our ERP contract, I am not adding another system." "How do I know the savings are real?" "I need to see a case study from a company like ours." "Our Operations Manager handles procurement and he says it is fine."

#### Define the Buyers (B2B)

- **Champion:** Karel (the Operations Manager) initiates, but Elena needs her own reason to support the purchase. The cost-savings data is her motivation.
- **Influencer:** External auditors or consultants who flag procurement as an area for improvement. The Operations Manager's recommendation carries weight.
- **Decision Maker:** Elena herself. At this company size, she has authority over operational software spend.
- **Budget Holder:** Elena.
- **Saboteur:** The ERP vendor (who may claim their system can do procurement analytics), or the Operations Manager himself if he feels threatened by transparency into his processes.
- **End User:** Not Elena. She would review dashboards and reports, but the day-to-day user is the Operations Manager or a procurement coordinator.

#### Channels

- Harder to reach than Karel. Finance Directors do not attend procurement events.
- Reached indirectly: Karel champions the tool, Elena approves based on ROI data.
- CFO peer networks and roundtables (for direct outreach)
- LinkedIn content targeting Finance Directors with cost-reduction messaging
- Case studies and ROI calculators shared during the sales process (Elena is reached during the deal, not before it)

### "Change-Resistant Tomasz"

**Buying Role:** Saboteur
**Early or Ideal Customer:** N/A (not a buyer, but must be navigated)

#### Define the Customer

- **Role/Title:** IT Manager (or Head of IT at smaller companies)
- **Firmographics:** Manufacturing or retail SME, 100-500 employees, European. Manages a small IT team (1-3 people) responsible for ERP, network infrastructure, and all business software. Already stretched thin supporting existing systems.
- **Role in Purchase:** Saboteur. Tomasz does not initiate the purchase and does not benefit from it. But any new software that touches company data, integrates with the ERP, or requires IT support lands on his desk. He can slow or block the purchase through technical objections.
- **Trigger Event:** A purchase request arrives for a cloud-based procurement tool he has never heard of from a startup. His concerns are immediate: integration, data security, another system to support, and another vendor to manage.
- **Where They Hang Out:** Not relevant for outreach. Encountered during the sales process when IT evaluation is required.
- **Who Influences Them:** ERP vendor technical support, IT peer networks, vendor security certifications and documentation, internal IT policies.

#### Define the Use Case

- **Situation Before Your Product:** Tomasz manages a stable (if limited) set of business systems. ERP, email, file storage, maybe a CRM. He has spent years getting these systems working together. Any new tool is a risk to that stability.
- **Current Solution:** The current procurement process (spreadsheets and email) does not involve IT at all. That is actually fine from Tomasz's perspective. No system means no integration work, no support tickets, and no security risk.
- **Frustrations with Current Approach:** He is not frustrated with the current approach. The Operations Manager is frustrated. Tomasz's frustration is with being asked to evaluate and support yet another cloud tool when he is already overloaded.
- **Consequences:** If he approves a tool that causes a data breach or integration failure, it reflects on him. If he blocks a tool the Operations Manager and Finance Director want, he is seen as an obstacle.
- **Past Spend:** Not applicable. Tomasz does not control procurement budgets.

#### Define the Problem

- **Jobs to Be Done:**
  1. Ensure any new software does not create security risks (data handling, access controls, vendor reliability)
  2. Ensure any new software integrates with existing systems without creating additional IT support burden
  3. Protect the stability of the existing IT environment
- **Pain Points (ranked by severity):**
  1. Pressure to approve new tools quickly while his concerns about security and integration are dismissed as "IT being difficult." (Major)
  2. No time to properly evaluate new vendors. His team is already fully committed to maintaining existing systems. (Major)
  3. Worried about data handling. Procurement data includes supplier contracts, pricing, and internal cost structures. Where is it stored? Who has access? What happens if the startup folds? (Major)
- **How Often They Feel This Pain:** Infrequently. Only when new software purchases are proposed. But each instance creates a multi-week evaluation process.
- **Problem Awareness:** Low. Tomasz does not see himself as having a problem. He is doing his job: protecting the company's IT environment. The startup needs to make his evaluation easy, not convince him he has a problem.
- **Why They Would Say No:** "Where is the data hosted?" "Does it integrate with our ERP?" "What certifications do you have?" "Who supports this if something breaks?" "We do not have capacity to onboard another system right now." "What happens to our data if your company shuts down?"

#### Define the Buyers (B2B)

- **Champion:** None. Tomasz does not champion software purchases.
- **Influencer:** Tomasz himself (can influence through technical objections and security requirements).
- **Decision Maker:** Not Tomasz, but he has effective veto power on technical grounds.
- **Budget Holder:** Not Tomasz.
- **Saboteur:** Tomasz IS the saboteur in this scenario.
- **End User:** Not Tomasz.

#### Channels

- Not relevant for outreach. Encountered during the sales process. The strategy is to prepare technical documentation, security FAQs, and integration guides that answer his objections before he raises them.

## Evidence Assessment

- **Overall rating:** Moderate (for Karel), Weak (for Elena), Assumption-Heavy (for Tomasz and the buyer map beyond Karel)
- **Strongest evidence:** The Operations Manager persona (Karel) is grounded in 4 of 6 discovery interviews. The spreadsheet-based procurement process, the lack of spend visibility, the cost pressure trigger, and the time drain are consistent themes across multiple conversations.
- **Biggest assumption:** The Finance Director persona (Elena) is largely inferred from how operations managers describe the approval process, not from direct conversations with finance decision-makers. If Elena does not see procurement as her problem to solve, the sales cycle stalls because there is no internal champion above Karel.
- **Evidence gaps:** No interviews with Finance Directors, CFOs, or company owners. No interviews with IT managers. The buyer map above the Operations Manager level is assumption-heavy. The pricing sensitivity ("we cannot afford another SaaS tool") needs testing with actual pricing conversations, not just interview sentiment.

## Next Steps

1. Interview 3-5 Finance Directors or CFOs at manufacturing SMEs in the target segment. Specifically test whether they see procurement as a cost reduction opportunity they would invest in, or whether it is delegated entirely to operations with no budget for tools. This is the single most important validation gap.
2. Build a named target list of 80 manufacturing SMEs in the Czech Republic with 50-200 employees and EUR 5M+ in annual procurement spend. Use the Czech Manufacturing Association directory and trade event attendee lists. If the actual count is below 50, expand the beachhead to include Slovakia.
3. During the next 2-3 discovery conversations, ask operations managers directly: "If you wanted to buy a procurement tool for EUR 300/month, who would need to approve it and what would they ask?" This maps the real buyer journey without needing separate finance interviews.
4. Prepare a one-page technical FAQ covering data hosting, ERP integration, and data portability. Even though Tomasz is assumption-based, these objections are predictable. Having answers ready before the first paid sale removes a common blocker.
