# Sales Battlecard Coaching Guide

## Purpose

Help cybersecurity startup founders prepare for competitive conversations with prospects. When a prospect says "But we're already looking at [competitor]", the founder needs a thoughtful, honest response ready. Not a script. A response grounded in genuine understanding of where they win, where they lose, and what questions to ask.

This is simplified for pre-seed/seed founders doing founder-led sales. There is no sales team. The founder IS the sales team. Every competitive conversation is their conversation.

## When to Use a Battlecard

- Before any sales meeting or demo. Review the relevant competitor card and refresh your killer questions.
- When a prospect mentions a competitor by name. Pull the card, know your response.
- At conferences where competitors are present. Know their pitch so you can position against it naturally.
- During founder-led sales. At pre-seed/seed, this is every single sale. You do not have the luxury of a sales team that memorises playbooks. You need to internalise this yourself.

## Battlecard Template (One Per Competitor)

Work through each section with the founder. One competitor at a time. Do not try to cover five competitors in surface-level detail. Two or three done well is better than six done badly.

### a. Competitor Overview (2-3 sentences)

Who are they? What do they sell? Who do they serve? What is their funding or company size?

Keep this factual. No spin. The founder should be able to describe the competitor as accurately as the competitor describes themselves.

**Coaching prompt:** "Describe [competitor] as if you were their head of marketing. If you cannot do that, you do not understand them well enough to compete against them."

**Cybersecurity example:** "CrowdStrike is a publicly traded endpoint security company (NYSE: CRWD) with over $3B in annual revenue. Their Falcon platform provides cloud-native endpoint detection and response, threat intelligence, and managed hunting services. They primarily serve mid-market to enterprise customers across all industries."

### b. Their Positioning

How do they describe themselves? What language do they use on their website, in their pitch decks, in their sales conversations?

This is about understanding their narrative, not critiquing it. You need to know what the prospect has already heard before you walked in the room.

**Coaching prompt:** "Go to their website right now. Read their homepage headline and their 'About' page. What do they claim? What words do they repeat? What customer outcomes do they promise?"

### c. Where They Win

Be honest. This is the most important section. Acknowledging a competitor's genuine strengths builds YOUR credibility with the prospect. If you trash-talk a product the prospect already likes, you lose trust instantly.

**Coaching prompt:** "Where is [competitor] genuinely better than you today? Not where you plan to catch up. Where are they better right now? Be honest. If you cannot answer this, you are either lying to yourself or you have not done your homework."

**Cybersecurity example:** "KnowBe4 wins on brand recognition and content library size. They have 15,000+ phishing templates and years of training content. If a prospect's primary decision criterion is breadth of out-of-the-box content, KnowBe4 is hard to beat."

### d. Where We Win

Specific advantages against THIS competitor. Not generic product features. Advantages that are tied to outcomes the prospect cares about.

Use the ROI Ladder from the value proposition exercise. Do not stop at features. Push to outcomes and ROI.

**Coaching prompt:** "What can you deliver for the customer that [competitor] cannot? Be specific. Tie it to a measurable outcome. 'We are more innovative' is not an advantage. 'We reduce phishing simulation setup time from 2 hours to 5 minutes because of X' is an advantage."

**Cybersecurity example:** "Against KnowBe4, we win on contextual relevance. Our simulations use the prospect's actual email patterns, internal tools, and org structure. Result: 3x higher employee engagement with training because the simulations feel real, not generic."

### e. Common Objections and Responses

The specific things prospects say when they are considering or already using this competitor. 3-5 per competitor.

Each objection needs a response that is honest, specific, and backed by evidence where possible. "Trust me, we are better" is not a response.

**Coaching prompt:** "What has a prospect actually said to you about [competitor]? Not what you imagine they might say. What have they actually said in a real conversation?"

If the founder has never lost a deal to this competitor (or never been in a competitive deal), flag this. They are guessing, not preparing.

**Format for each objection:**

> **Objection:** "But [competitor] already does X..."
> **Response:** [Your honest, specific response with evidence]
> **Evidence strength:** [Strong/Moderate/Weak/Assumption]

**Cybersecurity examples:**

> **Objection:** "CrowdStrike already covers endpoint detection. Why do we need you?"
> **Response:** "CrowdStrike is excellent at endpoint detection. We are not replacing them. We sit alongside CrowdStrike and cover the gap they do not address: [specific gap, e.g., email-based social engineering that bypasses endpoint controls]. In our pilot with [customer], CrowdStrike caught 0 of 12 simulated social engineering attacks because they never hit the endpoint."
> **Evidence strength:** Strong (if based on actual pilot data) / Assumption (if hypothetical)

> **Objection:** "We already use KnowBe4 for security awareness training."
> **Response:** "Good. That means you already recognise the problem. The question is whether generic training is actually changing employee behaviour. Ask your team: what is your phish-click rate trend over the last 12 months? If it is flat despite training, the content is not landing. Our approach is different because [specific differentiator]."
> **Evidence strength:** Moderate (if based on industry data about flat click rates) / Assumption (if untested)

### f. Killer Questions

Questions to ask early in a sales conversation that frame the evaluation criteria in your favour. These expose competitor gaps without naming the competitor directly. The prospect draws their own conclusions.

Good killer questions are open-ended, problem-focused, and impossible for competitors to answer well.

**Coaching prompt:** "What question, asked in the first 10 minutes of a meeting, would make a prospect realise that [competitor] cannot deliver what they need? The question should not mention the competitor. It should surface a requirement that only you can meet."

**Cybersecurity examples:**
- "How do you currently measure whether your security awareness training is actually changing employee behaviour, versus just checking a compliance box?" (Exposes vendors who only track completion rates, not behaviour change.)
- "When you run phishing simulations, are they based on generic templates or on the actual attack patterns targeting your industry?" (Exposes vendors using one-size-fits-all content.)
- "How quickly can your current solution adapt when a new attack vector emerges? Days, weeks, or months?" (Exposes vendors with slow content update cycles.)
- "What happens when an employee fails a simulation? Is the follow-up personalised to what they missed, or is it the same generic training for everyone?" (Exposes vendors with no adaptive learning path.)

### g. Landmines

Ideas or concerns you plant early in the conversation that create doubt about a competitor's approach later. Landmines are subtle. They are not attacks. They are legitimate concerns that happen to be areas where competitors are weak.

**Coaching prompt:** "What is a genuine limitation of [competitor]'s approach that most prospects do not think to ask about? Something that only becomes a problem 6 months into using their product?"

**Cybersecurity examples:**
- "One thing worth asking any vendor: what does the integration look like with your existing SIEM? Some solutions require significant professional services to get data flowing. We built native integrations for [list], so deployment is same-day."
- "It is worth checking how pricing scales. Some vendors price per user, which sounds reasonable until you have 5,000 employees and the annual cost exceeds your entire security budget. We price per [alternative model] so costs stay predictable."

## The "Do Nothing" Battlecard

This is often the real competitor. Not another vendor. Inaction. The prospect decides to keep doing what they are doing today, which might be nothing at all.

At pre-seed/seed, you will lose more deals to "do nothing" than to any named competitor. This battlecard is mandatory.

### Why Prospects Choose Inaction

- **Budget:** "We do not have budget for this right now." (Translation: it is not a high enough priority to create budget.)
- **Complexity:** "We would need to get IT/legal/procurement involved." (Translation: the perceived effort of buying outweighs the perceived pain of the problem.)
- **"Good enough":** "Our current approach is working fine." (Translation: they have not measured how badly it is working, or the pain is not acute enough.)
- **Other priorities:** "We have bigger fires to fight." (Translation: your problem is real but not urgent enough to act on now.)
- **Risk aversion:** "What if it does not work? We would rather wait." (Translation: the cost of a failed purchase feels higher than the cost of inaction.)

### Creating Urgency Without Fearmongering

Do NOT use scare tactics. Cybersecurity vendors are notorious for this and buyers are numb to it. Instead, quantify the cost of the status quo.

**Approaches that work:**
- "What is the cost of your current process? Not in breach risk, in staff time, manual effort, and opportunity cost."
- "You mentioned your team spends X hours per week on [manual task]. Over 12 months, that is Y hours. What else could that team be doing?"
- "How many incidents did you have in the last 12 months that your current approach did not catch? What did each one cost to remediate?"
- "Your competitors in [industry] are already adopting [approach]. Not because they are more advanced, but because the regulatory environment is forcing it. NIS2/DORA timelines are not flexible."

**Approaches that fail:**
- "You WILL get breached if you do not act." (Fearmongering. They have heard this from every vendor.)
- "Our competitor's customer had a massive breach." (Gossip. Unprofessional.)
- Creating artificial deadlines. ("This price is only available until Friday." You are a startup, not a used car dealership.)

### Questions That Expose the Cost of Inaction

- "Walk me through what happens today when [specific security event] occurs. Step by step."
- "How long does that process take? Who is involved?"
- "What is the cost of that, in hours, money, or business disruption?"
- "Has that cost increased over the last 12 months? Do you expect it to increase next year?"
- "If nothing changes in your current approach, what does that look like in 12 months?"

### The "Do Nothing" Objection Responses

> **Objection:** "We do not have budget for this."
> **Response:** "Understood. Let me ask: is there budget for the problem this solves? If a [specific incident] costs you [estimated amount] to remediate, and you had [number] of those last year, the budget question becomes: can you afford NOT to address this?"

> **Objection:** "We are handling it with our existing tools."
> **Response:** "That is fair. Can you walk me through how? I ask because most teams we speak to have a process that works at their current scale but breaks when [specific trigger, e.g., headcount doubles, compliance audit arrives, attack volume increases]. Is that a concern for you?"

> **Objection:** "We will look at this next quarter."
> **Response:** "What changes next quarter that makes it a better time? If the answer is nothing, the risk is that next quarter becomes the quarter after. What would need to happen for this to become urgent now?"

## Coaching Questions (Sequential Flow)

Work through these in order with the founder. Do not skip ahead.

1. **Who are the top 3 competitors that prospects actually mention in sales conversations?** Not competitors you found by Googling. Competitors that real prospects have named. If the founder has not had enough sales conversations to answer this, flag it. They need more conversations before a battlecard is useful.

2. **Have you ever lost a deal to a competitor? Tell me what happened.** Specifics. Which competitor? What did the prospect say? At what stage did you lose? What could you have done differently? If they have never lost a deal, either they have not sold enough, or they are not tracking losses honestly.

3. **For each competitor: can you honestly describe where they are better than you?** This is the hardest question. If a founder cannot answer it, they have not done their competitive research. Or they are in the Ego Echo-Chamber. Push hard here.

4. **What question, asked early in a conversation, would make a prospect realise your competitor cannot deliver?** This tests whether the founder understands the competitor's weaknesses well enough to expose them without naming them. If they cannot come up with a question, they do not know the competitor well enough.

5. **Have you built a "do nothing" battlecard?** If not, start there. This is usually the most important one. Walk through the reasons prospects choose inaction and rehearse the responses.

6. **What evidence do you have for each claim in your battlecard?** Go through every "where we win" claim and every objection response. Rate each one: Strong / Moderate / Weak / Assumption. A battlecard full of assumptions is a fiction document, not a sales tool.

## Red Flags to Watch For

Challenge the founder immediately if you see any of these patterns:

- **"We have no competitors."** Every product has competitors. The competitor might be Excel, a manual process, an intern, or doing nothing. If a founder says they have no competitors, they have not talked to enough customers. This is a major red flag. Push: "What does your prospect do today without your product? That is your competitor."

- **Trash-talking competitors.** If a founder's battlecard reads like an attack ad, stop them. Buyers see through this instantly and it destroys credibility. Push: "If I were a prospect and I asked about [competitor], would your response make me trust you more or less? Honest acknowledgement of a competitor's strengths makes you look confident, not weak."

- **Claims with no evidence.** Every "where we win" claim needs evidence. "We are faster" means nothing. "Our median deployment time is 2 hours versus their typical 6-week professional services engagement, based on our last 5 pilots" means something. Push: "What is your evidence for this? Is this from a real competitive deal, or is this what you hope is true?"

- **No "do nothing" battlecard.** If the founder only prepared cards against named competitors, they have missed the biggest one. At pre-seed/seed, "do nothing" kills more deals than any vendor. Push: "Have you lost more deals to a competitor or to inaction? Build a card for whatever beats you most often."

- **Obviously leading killer questions.** Questions like "Don't you think it's important to have AI-powered threat detection?" are transparent and insulting to the buyer. Killer questions must be genuinely open-ended. They should surface a real requirement, not lead the prospect to a predetermined answer. Push: "If the prospect answered 'no' to that question, would the conversation still be useful? If not, it is a leading question, not a killer question."

- **Battlecard built from desk research only.** If every claim comes from reading competitor websites and none from actual competitive deals, the battlecard is theory, not practice. Flag it as Assumption-Heavy. Push: "This reads like a competitive analysis, not a battlecard. A battlecard comes from real sales conversations where you competed and won or lost. Go have those conversations."

- **Swamp of False Signals pattern.** Watch for founders who claim competitive wins based on weak signals. "The prospect said they preferred our demo" is not a win. A signed contract is a win. Reference the Commitment Ladder: where did the deal actually land?
