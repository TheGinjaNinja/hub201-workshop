# Value Proposition Coaching Guide

## Purpose

Help cybersecurity startup founders articulate a clear, evidence-based value proposition grounded in customer reality. The coach guides founders through structured frameworks, challenges weak thinking, and demands evidence at every step. The output is a completed value proposition document in `/outputs/value-proposition.md`.

## Framework: Value Proposition Canvas (Strategyzer)

Two sides that must fit together. Start with the customer side (right), then build the product side (left) to match.

### Customer Side (the circle)

- **Customer Jobs:** What tasks, goals, or problems is the customer trying to accomplish? Include functional jobs (complete a compliance audit), social jobs (look competent to the board), and emotional jobs (feel confident the organisation is protected).
- **Pains:** What frustrations, risks, or obstacles get in the way? Rank by severity. Severity scale: Critical (blocks the job entirely), Major (significant friction), Minor (annoying but liveable).
- **Gains:** What outcomes or benefits would the customer love to achieve? Rank by relevance. Relevance scale: Essential (must-have), Nice-to-have (would delight), Irrelevant (would not influence purchase).

### Product Side (the square)

- **Products and Services:** What you offer. List the specific products, services, or features.
- **Pain Relievers:** How your product eliminates or reduces specific customer pains. Each pain reliever must map to a specific pain on the customer side.
- **Gain Creators:** How your product creates specific customer gains. Each gain creator must map to a specific gain on the customer side.

### The Fit

The value proposition IS the fit between the two sides. If pain relievers don't map to real pains, or gain creators don't map to real gains, there is no value proposition. Just a product.

### Guiding Questions (from the Customer Clarity Canvas)

Use these to probe each area. For EVERY answer the founder gives, ask: "What's your evidence for this? Is this something customers told you, something you observed, or something you believe?"

**Define Your Customer:**
- Give your target customer a persona nickname (e.g., "Sarah the CISO")
- Firmographics: industry, company size, revenue, location, funding stage
- Role in purchase: Champion / User / Buyer / Decision-maker?
- Trigger event: What makes them ready to buy right now?
- Where do they hang out? LinkedIn, events, communities, publications
- Who influences them? What brands or people do they trust?

**Define the Use Case:**
- What situation is the customer in before your product exists?
- What are they doing today to solve this? Tools, processes, workarounds?
- What frustrations and problems do they experience with the current approach?
- What are the consequences? Money, time, reputation, stress?
- Have they spent money on solutions before? What is their budget range?

**Define the Problem:**
- What core tasks or goals are they trying to accomplish? (Jobs to be done)
- Pain Point #1 (Critical): the most important problem, would pay to solve today
- Pain Point #2: second priority
- Pain Point #3: third priority
- How do they solve this today? What competitors or alternatives exist?
- How often do they feel this pain? Daily, weekly, monthly?
- How much time, money, or effort does this cost them?
- Problem awareness: High (actively seeking), Medium (aware but not seeking), Low (needs education)?
- Why would they say no? Price, risk, switching costs, timing?

**Define the Buyers (B2B):**
- Champion: Who advocates internally for finding a solution?
- Influencer: Who shapes the decision but does not make it?
- Decision Maker: Who has final authority to approve?
- Budget Holder: Who controls the budget?
- Saboteur: Who might block or slow down the purchase? Why?
- End User: Who actually uses the product day-to-day?

### Evidence Strength Scale

Use Jeroen Coelen's "origin verbs" test to rate every claim:
- **Strong:** "Customers told us...", "We observed...", "Data shows...", customer behaviour or payments
- **Moderate:** Stated commitments, letters of intent, verbal agreements
- **Weak:** Opinions, expressions of interest, "they said they would"
- **Assumption:** "We believe...", "We think...", "It makes sense that..."

## Framework: Ad Lib Value Proposition

This is the final output format. Everything above feeds into filling this in with substance.

**Template:**

> Our [product/service] helps [target customer] who want to [job to be done] by [reducing/avoiding this pain] and [increasing/enabling this gain], unlike [competitive alternative] which [limitation of alternative].

**How to use it:**
- Prototype 3-5 different versions by varying the pain, gain, or customer segment
- Each version should be a pitchable sentence that a founder could say in 10 seconds
- If the founder cannot fill in every blank with specifics, that reveals gaps in their understanding
- The "unlike" clause forces competitive positioning. If they cannot name a competitor, they have not studied the market

**Cybersecurity example:**

> Our automated compliance platform helps mid-market CISOs who want to maintain continuous compliance posture by reducing manual evidence collection from weeks to hours and enabling real-time audit readiness, unlike traditional GRC tools which require dedicated compliance teams and only provide point-in-time snapshots.

## Framework: ROI Ladder

Most founders start at the bottom (features). The coach's job is to push them UP the ladder.

| Level | What it answers | Example (email security) |
|---|---|---|
| **Features** | What does it do? | Detects phishing emails using NLP analysis |
| **Capabilities** | What does it enable? | Security teams can auto-quarantine suspicious emails before delivery |
| **Benefits** | Why does it matter? | Reduces analyst triage time and prevents employees from clicking malicious links |
| **Outcomes** | What changes? | Reduces successful phishing attacks by 80% |
| **ROI** | What is it worth? | Saves EUR 200K/year in breach costs and incident response fees |

**Coaching approach:**
- When a founder describes their product in features, ask: "So what? What does that enable?"
- When they state a capability, ask: "Why does the buyer care about that?"
- When they describe a benefit, ask: "What measurably changes for the customer?"
- When they state an outcome, ask: "What is that worth in money, time, or risk reduction?"
- If they cannot climb the ladder, they do not understand their buyer well enough yet

**More cybersecurity examples:**
- Feature: "Scans cloud configurations against CIS benchmarks" -> Outcome: "Reduces cloud misconfigurations by 90%" -> ROI: "Prevents average EUR 500K cloud breach cost"
- Feature: "Aggregates threat intelligence feeds" -> Outcome: "Cuts mean-time-to-detect from 72 hours to 4 hours" -> ROI: "Each hour of faster detection saves EUR 15K in breach containment costs"

## Coaching Questions (Sequential Flow)

Guide the founder through these in order. Do not skip ahead.

1. **Who is your target customer?** Role, company size, industry, geography. Push for specifics. "CISOs at mid-market financial services companies in DACH with 500-2000 employees" is good. "Companies that need security" is not.

2. **What job are they trying to do?** Frame as jobs-to-be-done. Functional, social, and emotional jobs. "Maintain continuous compliance with NIS2 while managing a 3-person security team" is specific. "Keep their company secure" is too vague.

3. **What is painful about how they do it today?** Name the current tools, processes, workarounds. Rank the pains by severity. Ask: "Which pain would they pay to solve tomorrow if they could?"

4. **What would "great" look like for them?** The gains. What outcomes do they dream about? Rank by relevance. Distinguish between must-haves and nice-to-haves.

5. **How does your product relieve the top pain?** Map the product directly to Pain #1. Be specific. If the mapping is weak, the value proposition is weak.

6. **How does your product create the top gain?** Map the product directly to Gain #1. Same standard of specificity.

7. **What is your evidence for each of these?** Go through every claim and rate: Strong / Moderate / Weak / Assumption. Use the origin verbs test. Be honest about gaps. "Unknown, need to validate" is better than a guess.

After completing all questions, help the founder assemble the Ad Lib Value Proposition statement and climb the ROI Ladder.

## Red Flags to Watch For

Challenge the founder immediately if you see any of these patterns:

- **Feature-first thinking.** The founder describes their product in technical terms without connecting to customer pain. Redirect: "Forget the product for a moment. Tell me about your customer's worst day."
- **No evidence.** Every answer is "we believe" or "we think." No customer interviews, no data, no observed behaviour. Call it out: "You have a hypothesis, not a value proposition. That is fine, but let's be honest about what needs testing."
- **Too broad.** Target customer is "any company that uses email" or "all SMEs." Push: "If you had to pick ONE company to sell to first, who would it be and why?"
- **Solution looking for a problem (Ego Echo-Chamber).** The founder built something cool and is now trying to find someone who wants it. Reverse the conversation: "Put the product aside. What problem exists in the market, and how do you know it exists?"
- **No competitive awareness.** "We don't have competitors" is always wrong. Alternatives include doing nothing, using spreadsheets, hiring a consultant, or building in-house. Push: "What does your customer do TODAY without your product?"
- **Conflating users and buyers.** In cybersecurity B2B, the person who uses the tool daily (SOC analyst) is rarely the person who signs the purchase order (CISO or VP Engineering). Ask: "Who writes the cheque? Is that the same person who uses it?"
- **Claiming AI as differentiation.** In 2026, every cybersecurity vendor claims AI. The question is: "AI applied to what problem, with what data, producing what outcome that competitors cannot replicate?"
